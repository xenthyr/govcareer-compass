GovCareer Compass — UX Principles

Document status: Permanent
Document version: 1.0.0

---

1. Core UX Principle

The user should never feel that they are navigating a government database alone.

The interface must progressively answer:

What can I do?
       ↓
What suits me?
       ↓
Why?
       ↓
What should I do next?

---

2. User Modes

The platform serves two major user types.

Explorer

Knows what they want.

Use:

- search;
- exam explorer;
- job explorer;
- comparison.

Seeker

Does not know what they want.

Use:

- Career Finder;
- preference assessment;
- recommendation system.

Both modes must remain available.

---

3. Homepage Principle

The homepage should communicate the problem quickly.

Core message:

«Government exams are not one-size-fits-all.»

The homepage should then provide:

Find My Career
Search Exams
Explore Jobs
Compare Careers
Ask Career AI

---

4. Information Hierarchy

Primary:

- search;
- Career Finder;
- eligibility;
- salary;
- job profile;
- family;
- location;
- comparison.

Secondary:

- promotion;
- housing;
- benefits;
- preparation;
- source details.

Supporting:

- methodology;
- glossary;
- about;
- privacy.

---

5. Eligibility-First UX

When a user asks:

«“Can I apply?”»

the system must not bury the answer.

Show prominently:

DIRECTLY ELIGIBLE

or:

CONDITIONALLY ELIGIBLE

or:

NOT ELIGIBLE

Then explain why.

---

6. Recommendation UX

Every recommendation must answer:

Why?

Why the system likes the career for this user.

Why not higher?

What prevented a higher score.

Trade-off

What the user sacrifices by choosing it.

Evidence

Which information is official and which is analytical.

---

7. Score Transparency

Never show:

92%

without context.

Show:

92% Career Fit

Salary      9/10
Family      9/10
Location    8/10
Authority   7/10
Risk        2/10

Scores are decision-support indicators, not official rankings.

---

8. Negative Metric Rules

Metrics such as:

- stress;
- risk;
- transfer burden;
- night-duty burden

must state that higher values represent greater burden.

Do not use green/high visual emphasis merely because a number is large.

---

9. Fact vs Analysis UX

Use visible labels:

OFFICIAL FACT

Directly supported by authoritative documentation.

HISTORICAL FACT

Official information from an earlier period.

CURRENT ESTIMATE

Calculated or estimated from current information.

PRACTICAL ASSESSMENT

Analytical interpretation.

NOT PUBLICLY VERIFIED

No sufficient public verification found.

---

10. No Critical Hover Information

Important information must not exist only in:

- hover tooltips;
- charts;
- colour;
- animations.

Hover may enhance information, but text must remain accessible.

---

11. Progressive Disclosure

The system may use:

- accordions;
- tabs;
- drawers;
- modals;
- expandable tables.

However:

«Hiding information for presentation is allowed; removing information is not.»

---

12. Questionnaire UX

The Career Finder should:

- ask one conceptual question at a time where appropriate;
- show progress;
- explain why a question matters when useful;
- allow back;
- allow edit;
- preserve answers;
- avoid unnecessarily repeating questions;
- use branching;
- minimize cognitive overload.

---

13. Personal Information Principle

The platform should collect the minimum information required for career matching.

For optional personal questions:

- explain relevance when necessary;
- allow “Prefer not to say” when appropriate;
- avoid collecting unnecessary sensitive information;
- keep Version 1 preference data local.

---

14. Error UX

Never expose raw technical errors.

Bad:

TypeError: undefined is not a function

Preferred:

«Something went wrong while loading this information. Please try again.»

For no results:

«No careers match your current criteria.»

Then offer:

«Relax one or more preferences.»

---

15. Search UX

Search should accept natural terms such as:

- Kolkata Police SI;
- Level 7;
- BA English;
- family-friendly;
- no physical test;
- night duty;
- SSC CGL;
- railway;
- parents.

Results should clearly identify what matched.

---

16. Comparison UX

Comparison must make differences obvious.

The first column should contain the metric.

Positive metrics and burden metrics must be visually distinguished.

Do not imply:

«higher is always better.»

---

17. Mobile UX

Mobile users must receive complete information.

Tables may scroll horizontally.

Cards may stack.

Filters may become a drawer.

Navigation may become a hamburger menu.

Buttons must remain finger-friendly.

No critical data may be intentionally hidden merely because the device is small.

---

18. Accessibility

The platform should provide:

- semantic HTML;
- heading hierarchy;
- keyboard navigation;
- visible focus;
- accessible labels;
- sufficient contrast;
- screen-reader-friendly controls;
- accessible tables;
- accessible dialogs;
- reduced-motion support.

---

19. Trust UX

Sources should be visible near important claims.

The user should be able to understand:

«Where did this information come from?»

and:

«Is this official or analytical?»

---

20. User Control

Users should be able to:

- change state;
- change language;
- change theme;
- change preferences;
- reset assessment;
- bookmark careers;
- remove comparisons;
- change ranking priorities.

The system should not lock users into one recommendation.

---

21. Recommendation Disagreement

The platform should allow:

«“What if I care more about salary?”»

and:

«“What if I refuse all-India transfers?”»

Results should update accordingly.

This makes the recommendation engine educational rather than authoritative.

---

22. Emotional Tone

Government-exam candidates may already be anxious or overwhelmed.

The interface should therefore be:

- calm;
- confident;
- informative;
- non-judgmental;
- professional.

Avoid fear-based language such as:

«“You will fail.”»

Prefer:

«“This career may be a weaker fit for your current preferences.”»

---

23. Visual Language

The visual identity should be:

- premium;
- modern;
- colourful but restrained;
- glass-inspired;
- research-oriented;
- trustworthy.

Avoid:

- excessive neon;
- excessive gradients;
- oversized emoji;
- visual clutter;
- childish design;
- excessive animation.

---

24. Decision-Centred Design

Every major page should answer at least one practical question:

Can I apply?
What will I earn?
What will I do?
Where will I work?
How difficult is it?
How much authority is there?
What will happen to family life?
Can I stay near home?
What are the trade-offs?
What should I prepare next?

---

25. Permanent UX Principle

«The interface should reduce the cognitive burden of choosing a government career, not merely make a large amount of information look attractive.»
